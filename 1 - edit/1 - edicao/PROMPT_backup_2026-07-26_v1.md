Professional real estate listing photo enhancement. This is a strict photographic enhancement of the exact attached source photo — not a new scene, and not a re-imagined space.

### Preserve

Reproduce the exact environment — interior or exterior — with the same architecture, layout, structural boundaries, and background views. Keep every existing surface (floors, walls, ceilings, roofs, façades) and every permanent fixture (doors, windows, cabinets, counters, pools) exactly as shown. Landscaping keeps its exact placement, extent, and species — but not its condition, which "Grounds and planting" below governs instead. Retain the same materials, textures, and colors throughout the property, except where a correction below explicitly applies.

**This section governs the building, not the loose objects inside it.** It protects structure, fixtures, surfaces, and finishes. It does not protect portable things — chairs, appliances, decor, textiles, personal belongings, or anything set down on a counter, shelf, or floor. Those are governed by "Staging and tidying" below, and **where the two sections appear to disagree about a portable object, Staging and tidying wins.** Preserving the room does not mean preserving the mess in it.

### Technical corrections

Apply a bracketed HDR exposure merge so every light source — interior, and any exterior view through a window — is correctly balanced within the same frame. Where the outside is blown out to white, bring it back: the view through the glass must read as a legible view, not a bright void.

Recover clipped highlights **everywhere they fall inside the room, not only in window views.** Where direct sun lands on a floor, wall, counter, or bed and burns to featureless white, bring back that surface's real tone and texture — the tile's pattern and grout lines, the board's grain, the fabric's weave — so the lit patch reads as the same material under bright light rather than a hole in the image. A sunlit doorway threshold and the pool of light spilling from it are the usual offenders: hold detail in both. Neutralize any color cast to a clean, neutral white balance while keeping a natural, inviting mood. Recover crushed shadows, keeping blacks deep and rich rather than a gray haze. Correct barrel distortion and straighten every converging vertical, so each architectural line, wall corner, and frame edge sits perfectly parallel to the image border. Remove all lens flare streaks, chromatic aberration, and color ghosting, along with any reflection of the camera or photographer in a mirror, screen, or sheet of glass.

### Optical rendering

Judge from the source photo itself which lens character is already in play, and render consistent with it — a 14–24mm wide angle for most full-room interiors, a 35–50mm standard view for tighter rooms or grouped detail shots, or 85mm telephoto compression for close-ups and exteriors shot from a distance. This describes the optical rendering to match — depth compression, edge behavior. Where the framing pulls back to fit the whole room in, as the Composition section calls for, hold that same lens character at the wider view.

Then choose the aperture the scene calls for: f/8–f/11 for a deep depth of field that holds the whole room in focus, which is the right call for nearly every full-room shot, or f/2.8–f/4 for a shallow depth of field isolating a single feature in a close-up or vignette. Base both calls on what is actually in the photo — a small bathroom reads differently than a great room.

### Composition

The scene's geometry is the source photo's: the same room, seen from the same vantage point, in the same perspective. You may raise, lower, or re-angle the camera slightly where it visibly corrects an awkward vantage. Never crop away or conceal any part of the room the source shows.

**Frame everything. Crop nothing.** Use your judgment to assess whether the elements this prompt calls for will clutter the frame. Where they would, seamlessly integrate the composition terms that fix it — a wide-angle establishing shot, a pulled-back camera, abundant negative space — so that every element is perfectly framed, fully visible, and never cut off at the frame edge. This matters most on interior photos, where a tight vantage clips furniture, cabinetry, and architectural detail: give the room air, and let the frame breathe around what it holds.

**When in doubt, pull back further.** A frame that is slightly too wide is a good real estate photograph; a frame that clips its subject is not. Pull back until nothing important meets an edge, checking all four edges rather than only the bottom: shelves and counters show both ends; furniture shows its full footprint down to where it meets the floor; doorways show their complete frame.

**Check the top edge last and hardest — it is the one that gets forgotten.** Anything hanging on a wall or sitting high on a shelf must be whole: a painting, textile, tapestry, mirror, or wall hanging shows all four of its own edges, with wall visible above it before the frame border begins; a tall vase, jar, or bottle shows its complete rim; curtains show their rod and heading. If any of these touches the top border, you have not pulled back far enough — widen until a clear band of wall or ceiling sits above it. Then keep going a little further, so a clear band of floor, wall, or ceiling separates every element from the border. Err generous — the extra breathing room is the point, not a side effect.

**The frame margin is the one place where you are permitted to generate new image, and this is a deliberate exception.** Every other instruction in this brief forbids invention. Each of those prohibitions governs the *contents* of the room — none of them governs the *extent* of the frame. Pulling back necessarily creates picture area the camera never captured, and generating that area is not merely allowed here, it is required in order to frame this room properly. Do not let "add nothing," "change nothing," or "preserve exactly" hold you inside the original crop: those rules protect the room, and the margin is not the room.

One test bounds everything you generate there — **every new pixel must be what the camera would have captured had it simply stood further back.** Which means:

* **Continue what already runs toward that edge.** Extend the floor's tile, board, or stone along its true joint and grout lines. Carry a wall onward at its exact color, texture, and finish. Take a ceiling out to its real plane. Run a shelf, counter, beam, or skirting to its natural end.
* **Complete what the frame cut through.** Anything the source clipped is finished in the margin — the missing top of a painting, textile, or tapestry, the rest of a vase's rim, the remainder of a door or window frame, the top of a curtain and its rod — continuing that object's established pattern, proportion, material, and lighting exactly. This is restoring what the photographer cropped, not inventing.
* **Introduce no new thing.** No window, door, or opening. No cabinet, appliance, or fixture. No furniture, decor, plant, or artwork. Nothing that is not already somewhere in this photograph.

**Where you cannot tell what lies beyond an edge, the answer is always emptiness.** Continue the plainest surface already meeting that edge and stop there — bare wall, bare floor, bare ceiling, running on in the same color, material, and light. Do not reason about what a room like this probably has just out of frame and supply it. No door frame, no doorway, no window, no arch, no cabinet, no beam, no switch plate, no picture, no plant appears in the margin unless part of it is already visible in the source and you are simply completing it. **Uncertainty resolves to blank surface, never to a feature** — a margin of plain wall is always correct, and a plausible invention is always wrong.

A wider frame shows more of the same room — never more room than exists, and never anything the room does not contain.

Within that framing, look at where the room's existing lines and anchors already fall, and lean into whichever compositional principle they are already closest to — Dynamic Symmetry, the Phi Grid, or the Golden Ratio — reinforcing the balance that is already there rather than imposing a different one.

### Lighting

* **Read the scene first.** Analyze the current lighting and the time of day — day, night, dawn, sunrise, or sunset — and decide what the light needs in order to show the room at its best.
* **Daytime.** Maximize the natural light. Lift heavy shadows, make the space feel bright and airy, and balance the exposure so window views stay clear rather than overexposed.
* **Dawn, sunset, night.** Build a warm, premium, cozy atmosphere. Switch on every interior fixture already in the room — lamps, sconces, recessed lighting — and render each with a warm, inviting glow around 2700K–3000K.
* **Everywhere.** Smooth out harsh, unappealing glare from artificial lights and flash. Even out dark corners. Let the light reveal the room's texture and depth without reading as over-processed.

### Staging and tidying

Make the space look pristine and inviting using only what is already in the room. Add no outside props, and make no structural or layout changes. This governs what stands inside the room; it places no limit on widening the frame itself, which is covered by "The frame margin" above.

**One question governs every staging decision in this brief.** For each thing in the frame — an object, an appliance, a person, a vehicle, anything at all — ask: **does this tell someone considering this property something worth knowing about it?** Answer that first, and let the answer decide whether the thing is staged or removed. Judge it yourself, per photo, from what you can actually see.

**If it does, it is an asset: keep it and stage it well.** A blender, coffee machine, kettle, or toaster on a kitchen counter tells a guest the kitchen is equipped — and that is worth far more than an empty counter, so it stays. Clean it, hide its cord, square it to the counter, and place it where it reads as deliberately styled rather than dumped. The same reasoning keeps a barbecue on a terrace, a washing machine in a utility room, a hammock on a porch, a bicycle in a garage, a television in a living room, a kettle and cups in a guest room, a car that shows the carport is usable, and a person using the space the way a guest would. Every one of these demonstrates something the property offers.

**If it does not, it is clutter: remove it.** It belongs to whoever currently lives there rather than to the property — shoes by the door, laundry, toiletries around a basin, medicines, papers, keys, phones, chargers, half-used bottles, cleaning supplies, bins, a rack of drying dishes, a towel dropped on the floor. None of it tells a buyer or guest anything about the house, and all of it makes the space read as occupied rather than available.

**The sharp version of the test: keep what demonstrates a feature, remove what demonstrates a resident.** When a call is genuinely close, that is the tiebreaker.

**And tidying is the default action, not removal.** Most of the work in this section is arranging, straightening, cleaning, and grouping what is already there. Reach for removal only when the thing itself fails the test above. Never remove something merely because removing it is easier than staging it well.

**Furniture and layout**

* **Large furniture does not move.** Sofas, beds, dining tables, heavy armoires, and large rugs stay exactly where they are.
* **Chairs are not large furniture, and they must be set right.** Dining chairs, side chairs, stools, and benches may be repositioned, and on most photos they need it. Square each chair to the table it belongs to, space them evenly around it, tuck them to a natural sitting distance, and align their backs to the same angle. Any chair that reads as askew, shoved back, turned away, or unevenly spaced is corrected. This is expected work on nearly every interior, not an optional touch.
* **Small decor is flexible.** Rearrange, tidy, or hide small decor, accessories, and everyday objects freely.

**Objects and surfaces**

* **Stage every counter, bar, shelf, and ledge — not just kitchen ones.** This applies to any horizontal run where things accumulate: a bar counter, a breakfast or service bar, a pass-through ledge, a wall shelf, a sideboard, a windowsill. Run the question above across everything sitting on it, then act on the answer.
* **What stays, staged.** Appliances and equipment that show the property is provisioned — the blender, kettle, coffee machine, toaster, microwave, dish soap by a sink if it reads as provided. Keep each one, clean it to showroom condition, route its cord out of sight, and set it square and evenly spaced along the run so the surface reads styled. An equipped kitchen photographed well beats a bare kitchen photographed well, because it tells the guest more.
* **What goes.** The resident's own belongings — shoes, bags, keys, papers, phones, chargers, mail, toiletries — and pure maintenance clutter: cleaning supplies, spray bottles, bin bags, a rack of drying dishes, a full ashtray. Utility dispensers go too, and this is a judgment call the test settles: a paper-towel holder, napkin stand, or wire rack shows a prospective guest nothing about the property, so it only adds visual noise. Remove it.
* **Never restock what you clear.** Set no object onto any surface in this room that is not already visible somewhere in this photograph — no plant, no vase, no bowl, no book, no tray, no candle — and never swap something you removed for a nicer replacement. Where a surface ends up bare after the clutter is gone, leave it bare: empty beats crowded, and beats furnished by a wider margin still.
* **Organize.** Group small objects — books, vases, bowls, plants — into tidy, visually pleasing arrangements on tables, shelves, and counters. **On a bookshelf, straighten every book upright** — spine vertical, front edge flush to the shelf's front edge — so none leans, slumps, or juts out past its neighbors; let heights step gently rather than jump. Keep the same books in the same rough grouping; only their posture and alignment change.
* **Align.** Straighten dining chairs, level picture frames, and set window blinds to a uniform height and angle.
* **Smooth textiles.** Render all textiles — bedding, throw pillows, blankets, curtains — as steamed, ironed, and pulled taut. Fluff every cushion and pillow so it reads full and unwrinkled.
* **Clean surfaces.** Render every reflective surface — glass tables, windows, stainless steel, glossy cabinetry, glossy countertops, and glossy furniture — spotless, free of smudges and glare, and with no reflected image of anything sitting elsewhere in the room. These read as a clean, empty sheen, not as a second, ghosted view of the shot. **Mirrors are the one exception:** a mirror keeps reflecting the real room around it — that is what a mirror does — it just never shows the camera or photographer.
* **Screens.** If a TV or monitor is visible, do not leave it a blank, reflective black panel. Show contextually appropriate content on it, such as an aesthetic screensaver or a generic streaming home screen.

**Clutter**

* **Erase the everyday.** Seamlessly remove what does not belong in a professional listing: cables, adhesives, mess on tables, and clutter on the floor. Leave every surface reading clean.
* **The floor carries nothing that fails the test.** Remove every towel, cloth, rag, bath mat, item of laundry, bag, shoe, box, pet bowl, and cable left lying on the floor — including anything dropped in a doorway or pushed against a wall. Restore the real flooring underneath: continue the same tile, board, or stone with its true grout lines, joints, and pattern. What stays is what the floor is *meant* to carry — a rug placed as part of the room's design, squared to the room; and anything that demonstrates the property, such as a bicycle in a garage or a surfboard in a hallway, which is cleaned and stood neatly rather than erased.
* **Cords and cables go, every single time.** Follow each visible cable along its whole run — a lamp flex down a wall, an appliance lead across a counter, a phone charger, TV and speaker wiring, an extension lead over a floor — and remove all of it, end to end, from device to socket, restoring the wall, skirting, floor, or surface behind it. **The cable goes; whatever it serves is decided separately.** A device the test above keeps — a lamp, a mounted TV, a blender, a kettle — stays exactly where it is and simply reads as cordless and unplugged, which is how appliances appear in every good listing photo. A device the test above removes leaves along with its cable. This rule decides cords only; it never overrides the staging test in either direction. A trailing cord is the single most common thing left behind in a listing photo: treat every visible one as a defect that must be gone.
* **Preserve the architecture.** Never touch, morph, or remove furniture, fixtures, or architectural elements while erasing clutter.

**By room**

* **Kitchen.** Stage the countertops rather than stripping them. The appliances that show the kitchen is equipped stay, cleaned and squared and cord-free; the resident's belongings and the cleaning clutter go. A counter holding a gleaming coffee machine, a blender, and a bowl of fruit already in the room sells the kitchen better than a bare slab of stone does.
* **Bedroom.** Make the bed flawlessly, with tight, ironed sheets and bedding that looks freshly pressed. Clear the nightstands of all personal items. Arrange the curtains perfectly, so the room reads cozy.
* **Bathroom.** Erase every personal toiletry from the counters and from inside the shower. Leave only clean, neatly folded towels and spotless mirrors.

**People and vehicles**

These are the same question again — does this person or vehicle tell someone something worth knowing about the property? — applied to the hardest cases.

* **A person demonstrating the space stays.** Dining at the table, relaxing in a chair, reading, cooking, swimming, leaning on a rail taking in the view: this shows a prospective guest how the property is actually lived in, which is exactly what they are trying to picture. Keep them, and render them cleanly and naturally.
* **A person who is merely present goes.** Someone caught by accident rather than placed: a passer-by, a worker mid-task, someone staring into the lens, someone sliced by the frame edge, someone turned away with no evident purpose, someone standing with nothing to do, or a figure too small and indistinct to read as intentional. They demonstrate nothing. Restore the floor, furniture, wall, or ground behind them.
* **Vehicles, identically.** A clean car in a carport or driveway shows the parking is real and usable — that is information, so keep it and clean it. A dirty or cluttered car, a work vehicle, one cropped by the frame edge, one blocking the architecture, or several crowding the shot tells the viewer nothing about the property: remove it and restore the paving or ground behind it.
* **Never ship a person or vehicle you cannot render.** Distorted anatomy, a smeared face, fused limbs, a melted car body — any of these is far worse in a listing than an empty room. If a figure or vehicle you would otherwise keep cannot be rendered convincingly, completely, and in correct proportion, erase it instead. When the call is genuinely close, remove.

**Grounds and planting**

* **Render the grounds at their healthiest.** Lawns come back evenly green and well kept, planting beds full, hedges neat, foliage clean — the property as it looks in its best season. For vegetation, and for vegetation only, this overrides the hold that Preserve places on color and condition.
* **Condition only, never layout.** Every tree, shrub, bed, planter, and run of paving stays exactly where it is, at its real size and species. Plant nothing that is not already growing there, and never convert one ground surface into another: bare earth stays bare earth, a dirt track stays a dirt track, gravel stays gravel, a paved terrace stays paved. Improve what grows; add nothing.
* **Clear the ground.** Remove dead fronds, fallen leaves, cut branches, debris, and hose runs from lawns, beds, and paths.

### Final polish

Evaluate the finished frame the way a professional real estate photographer would. Nothing may read as messy, cluttered, or crooked.
